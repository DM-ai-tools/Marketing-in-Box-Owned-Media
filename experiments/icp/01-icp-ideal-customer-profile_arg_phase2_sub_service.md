1. Identity Snapshot
Marco Delic, 42, is a director of a small property development company operating around outer-metropolitan Melbourne — think Pakenham, Cranbourne, Clyde North corridors. His company runs two to three townhouse or small-lot subdivision projects a year, typically eight to eighteen dwellings per site, with project end values in the $6M–$15M range. He's not a first-time developer; he's done one or two deals before, usually funded through a major bank or a mortgage via his accountant's contact, but this is his first project needing a structured construction finance facility at this scale, with a build budget north of $30,000 in professional and establishment costs before a shovel hits the ground. He runs a lean operation — himself, a part-time project manager, an external quantity surveyor (QS) and a solicitor he trusts. He doesn't have an in-house finance manager; he wears that hat himself, which means his time is split between site, trades and lenders. He's comfortable reading a feasibility but gets uneasy once lender jargon (pre-sales covenants, QS progress certifications, GRV) enters the conversation. Procurement is informal: no tender process, no finance committee — the decision sits entirely with him, though he'll often run numbers past his accountant and sometimes his wife, who is a co-director.

2. Current Reality
Marco has land under contract or already settled, DA (development approval) either issued or close to issued, and he knows he needs a construction facility to fund the build. He's spoken to his existing bank and either been declined, offered a lower Loan-to-Cost Ratio (LCR) than he needs, or given terms with pre-sale conditions he can't realistically meet in the current market. He now understands, in broad terms, that "non-bank lenders" or "specialist development finance" exist as a category — he's heard the names Liberty, La Trobe, or similar mentioned by a colleague or on a property forum — but he doesn't know which lender suits his deal, how their terms actually differ, or how to approach them directly. He's at the stage of googling things like "construction finance broker Melbourne" or "development finance for small developers," comparing broker websites, trying to work out who actually understands a project his size versus who only handles $50M+ apartment towers or, conversely, only does vanilla home loans.

4. What's Actually True Under the Hood
Marco is more exposed than he lets on. His deposit and equity are often tied up in the land and soft costs, so a finance delay directly threatens his settlement timeline and could trigger penalty interest or loss of the site. He's acutely aware that one bad project — a blown budget, a finance knockback, a lender dispute mid-build — could damage his reputation with the trades, agents and the handful of private investors he sometimes brings in on JV terms. He doesn't fully understand progressive drawdown mechanics (how funds release against QS-certified stages of construction) and is quietly anxious that he'll run short of cash mid-build if drawdowns don't line up with his payment obligations to the builder. He also suspects, correctly, that dealing with multiple lenders and comparing terms properly is beyond what he has time to do well himself, but he's reluctant to admit he needs that much hand-holding. Margin pressure is real: construction costs have moved, and a facility with unfavourable terms or slow drawdown turnaround can eat into what's left of his project margin.

5. Goals and Desired Outcomes
Functional Goals: Secure a construction finance facility sized appropriately to project cost, with workable loan-to-cost and pre-sale conditions, and understand exactly how and when funds will be drawn down against build stages.
Emotional Goals: Feel confident he's not missing a better deal elsewhere, reduce the anxiety of dealing directly with unfamiliar lenders, and feel like someone is genuinely on his side rather than just pushing him toward one panel lender.
Lifestyle/Operational Goals: Free up his own time to focus on site and trades rather than chasing lenders, and build a repeatable finance relationship he can reuse on his next one or two projects a year without starting from scratch each time.

6. Pains, Fears, and Risks
He fears a finance delay pushing back his build start, which has flow-on costs: holding costs on the land, extended interest, trades booked and waiting. He fears being locked into a facility with pre-sale or LVR (loan-to-value ratio) conditions he can't meet, forcing a fire-sale of stock or a scramble for mezzanine funding. He's concerned about reputational risk with his JV partner or investors if the project stalls due to a finance structuring error that a more experienced broker might have avoided. There's also opportunity cost: every week spent calling banks and getting knocked back is a week closer to a tightening finance market or rising rates eroding his margin further.

7. Problems They Face Getting What They Need
Marco struggles to tell the difference between brokers who genuinely specialise in development and construction finance versus generalist mortgage brokers who list it as one of many services. Lender websites and broker sites use similar language, so comparing who actually has non-bank panel access (versus who just has an "aggregator" relationship with the big four) is difficult from the outside. There's no simple way to compare indicative terms across lenders without approaching each one, which is time he doesn't have. He's unsure what proof of credibility to look for — years operating, deal size experience, lender relationships — and testimonials or case studies are rare or hard to verify in this niche. Once he does pick a broker, he worries about post-settlement support: who manages the drawdown process, QS liaison and lender reporting across the life of the build, since that's where he suspects problems actually surface.

8. Barriers and Uncertainties (Why they're stuck)

He's also unclear on broker fees and how a broker gets paid, and whether that creates a bias in which lender gets recommended.
